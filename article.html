Abstract

This study investigates the relationship between demographic and behavioural risk factors and healthcare insurance expenditure using interpretable regression modelling and structured model evaluation. Healthcare cost is reframed as a proportional indicator of cumulative physiological strain and functional burden rather than as an isolated financial outcome.

Exploratory analysis revealed substantial right-skewness in raw expenditure, motivating log-transformation to stabilise variance and enable proportional interpretation. A baseline multiple linear regression model explained approximately 76% of variance in log-transformed healthcare costs. Incorporating nonlinear age terms and interaction effects between smoking and BMI improved explanatory power to approximately 82%, indicating compounding behavioural–physiological risk patterns.

Smoking status was associated with approximately 88% higher expected healthcare expenditure, holding other variables constant, while age contributed approximately 3.5% incremental burden per year and BMI contributed approximately 2% per unit increase. Interaction terms demonstrated amplification of metabolic strain among smokers.

Predicted values were subsequently reframed as relative burden ratios centred around the population median to enhance interpretability across healthcare systems. A Random Forest benchmark produced only marginal performance gains, supporting the adequacy and transparency of structured regression modelling.

From an Occupational Therapy perspective, healthcare expenditure functions as a downstream indicator of participation restriction, endurance limitation, and sustained adaptive load. The findings support the use of interpretable predictive modelling to inform preventative intervention strategies while maintaining ethical responsibility and transparency.

1. Introduction: Healthcare Cost as a Marker of Occupational Strain

Healthcare expenditure is frequently analysed as a financial endpoint. In this study, cost is conceptualised as a measurable downstream indicator of cumulative physiological strain and participation restriction. Rather than identifying individuals who “cost more,” the analytical objective is to examine how behavioural and demographic exposures compound over time to increase long-term burden.

From an Occupational Therapy (OT) perspective, healthcare cost is not merely the result of discrete medical events. It reflects progressive erosion of occupational endurance, increasing environmental adaptation demands, chronic self-management load, and narrowing participation capacity. Rising expenditure signals strain in the dynamic interaction between person, environment, and occupation.

Within the Person–Environment–Occupation (PEO) framework, health behaviours such as smoking interact with physiological variables such as BMI and age to influence occupational performance. The statistical modelling approach therefore serves as a quantitative lens through which these interactions can be formally evaluated.

2. Distributional Structure and Occupational Burden

Raw healthcare charges demonstrated substantial right skewness.

From a modelling standpoint, skewness necessitates transformation. From an OT standpoint, skewness suggests unequal distribution of sustained strain. A minority of individuals appear to carry disproportionate chronic management load, likely reflecting prolonged participation restriction and reduced occupational balance.

Log transformation stabilised variance and enabled proportional interpretation.

Conceptually, log transformation aligns with the idea that health burden accumulates multiplicatively rather than additively. Chronic strain compounds across time, and each additional stressor does not merely add cost — it increases vulnerability.

Smoking status demonstrated elevated median expenditure and increased dispersion. OT interpretation: smokers may experience both heightened baseline physiological strain and increased variability in participation stability, reflecting episodic exacerbations layered onto chronic compromise.

Age and BMI emerged as consistent predictors. These variables directly influence endurance, cardiometabolic efficiency, and capacity for sustained occupational engagement.

3. Baseline Regression and Quantification of Occupational Strain

The baseline log-linear model explained approximately 76% of variance in log-transformed cost (R² ≈ 0.76).

Age (β ≈ 0.034) implies a 3.5% increase in expected healthcare burden per additional year. Over 20 years, this compounds to approximately 100% increased expected cost, reflecting progressive endurance decline across the lifespan.

BMI (β ≈ 0.02) implies roughly 2% increased burden per BMI unit. A 10-unit BMI increase corresponds to approximately 22% increased expected expenditure. OT interpretation: incremental biomechanical and metabolic strain accumulates gradually, influencing sustained occupational capacity.

Smoking (β ≈ 0.63) implies approximately 88% higher expected healthcare cost. This reflects behavioural amplification of physiological stress.

Within MOHO (Model of Human Occupation), smoking may be interpreted as a volitional pattern interacting with performance capacity, influencing long-term health trajectories.

4. Nonlinear Age Effects and Life-Course Functional Trajectories

Quadratic age terms significantly improved adjusted R² to approximately 0.82.

The curvature suggests accelerated cost escalation during midlife, followed by stabilisation at persistently elevated levels.

OT interpretation: this reflects narrowing adaptive reserve. Early strain may be compensated for, but repeated exposure reduces occupational resilience.

5. Interaction Effects: Compounding Behavioural and Physiological Strain

The smoker × BMI interaction (β ≈ 0.05) indicates that the marginal effect of BMI increases by approximately 5% among smokers.

This suggests multiplicative strain: behavioural exposure amplifies biomechanical burden.

Within the Kawa model metaphor, smoking introduces turbulence that amplifies the impact of existing rocks (physiological stressors) in the river of life.

6. Model Diagnostics and Structural Validation

Residual diagnostics indicated approximate homoscedasticity. Durbin–Watson statistics approximated 2, suggesting absence of autocorrelation.

Condition number analysis indicated acceptable multicollinearity levels.

The predicted vs actual alignment suggests that cumulative burden is structurally captured by the nonlinear interaction model.

7. Relative Burden as Early Occupational Risk Indicator

Predicted values were reframed relative to median cost.

Smoking shifts the entire burden curve upward, indicating systemic amplification.

OT interpretation: relative burden can function as a population-level screening proxy for emerging participation restriction and escalating chronic strain.

8. Machine Learning Benchmark and Interpretability

Random Forest modelling produced marginal performance gains, suggesting the dominant risk structure is smooth and cumulative.

In healthcare contexts, interpretability is ethically essential. Transparent regression modelling supports clinical dialogue and preventative policy planning.

9. Conclusion: Data Science in Service of Occupational Health

Age contributes ~3.5% annual burden growth. BMI contributes compounding metabolic strain. Smoking produces ~88% higher expected cost and amplifies other risk factors.

From an Occupational Therapy perspective, healthcare expenditure emerges as a quantifiable manifestation of cumulative participation restriction and sustained adaptive load.

Predictive modelling, when ethically framed, can support early intervention, health promotion, and long-term functional sustainability.

10. Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged in interpreting these findings. First, the dataset reflects cross-sectional observations rather than longitudinal trajectories. As a result, age-related effects are inferred from between-individual variation rather than within-person change over time. True life-course burden accumulation would require repeated measures data to capture dynamic progression.

Second, healthcare expenditure is an indirect proxy for functional burden. Healthcare expenditure represents a system-level utilisation response and may only partially capture the lived experience of occupational disruption and participation restriction. While cost often correlates with disease severity and management intensity, it does not directly measure participation restriction, occupational balance, endurance limitation, or quality of life. From an Occupational Therapy perspective, expenditure captures system response rather than lived experience.

Third, unobserved confounders such as socioeconomic status, access to care, environmental barriers, and psychosocial stressors were not included in the model. These variables may mediate or moderate the relationships identified here.

Fourth, the use of a log-linear specification assumes proportional effects. While diagnostics supported model adequacy, real-world health processes may involve additional nonlinear thresholds not captured in this dataset.

Additionally, findings may not generalise across healthcare systems with substantially different reimbursement structures, insurance models, or access-to-care dynamics.

Finally, predictive modelling in healthcare must be interpreted ethically. These findings should not be used to penalise individuals or restrict access to care. The appropriate application is preventative planning, population-level insight, and early identification of emerging burden patterns.

11. Future Research Directions

Future research should prioritise longitudinal data collection to examine within-individual burden accumulation across time. Tracking functional status, healthcare utilisation, and participation metrics concurrently would enable direct testing of occupational strain models.

Incorporating psychosocial and environmental variables would allow deeper integration of OT theoretical frameworks such as PEO and MOHO. Variables capturing work demands, caregiving load, housing stability, and access to rehabilitation services would strengthen explanatory depth.

Methodologically, future studies could explore mixed-effects modelling, survival analysis for high-burden transitions, or causal inference techniques to distinguish correlation from directional influence.

From a healthcare systems perspective, relative burden modelling could be integrated into preventative screening frameworks. Individuals exhibiting accelerating proportional burden growth could be targeted for early occupational therapy intervention, lifestyle modification support, and environment-based adaptation planning.

Finally, interdisciplinary collaboration between data scientists, occupational therapists, actuaries, and public health practitioners would allow predictive modelling to inform policy while maintaining ethical integrity and patient-centred focus.
